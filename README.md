# Needed

*A manifesto for everyone with work left to do.*

---

## 1. What is happening

Someone you know lost their job this year, and it was not their fault.

Not laziness. Not a skills gap. Not a failure to "adapt." They were doing the
work, often doing it well, and the work changed underneath them faster than a
person can re-plan a life. A career is a mortgage, a city, a set of
relationships, and a self-image. It cannot be rebuilt on the timetable that
layoffs run on.

The arguments about how many jobs and how fast will keep going for years.
We are not waiting for the final accounting. We can see the shape of it
already: the tasks that were easiest to describe are the tasks that were
easiest to automate, and an enormous number of people spent their working lives
on exactly those tasks — not because they lacked ambition, but because those
were the jobs that existed.

The worst part is not the lost income, though that is bad enough. It is the
lost *use*. To be needed by other people is most of what dignity is made of.
When that is taken away, no amount of encouragement helps, because
encouragement is not a role.

## 2. What we won't do

**We won't take the blame.** Displacement is being narrated as a personal
failing — a test of resilience, a prompt to "upskill," a lesson about staying
relevant. That is a way of moving the cost onto the people who did not cause
it.

**We won't pick a side in a fake argument.** "AI will save us" and "AI will
destroy us" are both ways of not doing anything. One is a sales pitch, the
other is a comfort.

**We won't mistake an audience for a movement.** People who read, agree, and
share are not a movement. They are a mailing list with a mood.

**We won't wait for permission** — from governments, employers, foundations,
or the companies that built the thing that displaced us.

## 3. What we believe

1. **Intelligence is becoming cheap. Organization is not.** For the first
   time, capable models run on hardware ordinary people already own. The
   bottleneck is no longer who can afford a model. It is who can coordinate
   people, define a problem, and maintain an answer. That is work we can do.

2. **Usefulness is proof, not a feeling.** You do not become useful by being
   told you are. You become useful when someone specific depends on something
   you made, and notices when it breaks.

3. **What nobody can afford alone, we buy once and hold together.** Compute,
   storage, open-weight models, and the expertise to know whether they work.
   A crew of five paying its own way through all of that is a crew that dies
   when its budget does. Shared cost is not charity — it is the difference
   between a hobby and an institution.

4. **Own what you build.** Where someone can pay for the work, charge them and
   keep it. Cooperative ownership — the people doing the work holding the
   asset — beats both charity and being employed by a stranger. We give away
   the code and the knowledge. We keep the tools, the relationships, and the
   revenue.

5. **Small and real beats large and planned.** One tool that a hundred people
   depend on is worth more than a platform that will launch someday. We
   measure ourselves in working things, not roadmaps.

6. **Nobody gets left holding the bill.** Volunteer effort is a gift to a
   community, never a subsidy owed to an employer. Purpose is not a wage, and
   we will not let one be substituted for the other.

## 4. Why open weights change the arithmetic

The decisive fact of this moment is not that machines got smart. It is that
the smart part became *copyable*.

A model that runs on a laptop, a donated GPU, or a cheap rented hour is a
tool that cannot be withdrawn from you, priced against you, or licensed out
from under you. It belongs to whoever runs it. That is new, and it is the
foundation this whole thing stands on.

It also means the advantage shifts from *owning the machine* to *knowing what
to ask of it and being accountable for the answer*. That is a human skill, it
is unevenly distributed by luck rather than merit, and it can be taught to
each other for free. So we will.

## 5. What we build

Anything that meets one test: **a real person would notice if it disappeared.**

Not demos. Not papers. Not "proofs of concept." Working things, in the open,
usable by someone who is not a programmer.

The territory we care about is the territory no market will serve well,
because the people who need it cannot pay:

- **Claiming what you're owed.** Benefit navigators, unemployment and retraining
  paperwork, medical-billing error detection, tenancy and labor rights in plain
  language.
- **Local knowledge and language.** Translation, literacy, and accessibility
  tools for languages and disabilities that no product team will ever
  prioritize.
- **Holding institutions accountable.** Public procurement, budgets, permits,
  contracts — read at a scale no newsroom can staff anymore.
- **The small economy.** Bookkeeping, invoicing, and inventory for co-ops,
  repair shops, and one-person businesses that cannot afford software.
- **Repair, care, and maintenance.** The knowledge that used to live in
  someone's hands: fixing appliances, caring for elders, keeping a house alive.
- **Memory.** Local newspapers, oral histories, records that exist only on
  degrading media.
- **Teaching.** Tutoring for the children whose schools cannot staff it.

Every project ships with a name, a license, a public repository, a maintainer,
and a note saying who it is for. Where the people it serves can pay something,
it charges them and the crew keeps it.

## 6. How we organize

**Crews, not a company.** Three to seven people. Small enough that no one is
anonymous and no one is waiting on a decision.

**One shipped thing every thirty days.** A crew that has not put something in
front of a real user in a month is not a crew. It is a chat.

**Rotating roles.** Every crew needs someone who builds, someone who maintains,
someone who writes it down in plain language — and, most importantly, someone
whose only job is talking to the people it is for. That last role is the one
that gets skipped, and it is the one that decides whether the work was worth
doing.

**No permission required.** Start a crew. Choose a problem. Register it in the
open so others can find you and so two crews don't build the same thing badly.

**The Useful Commons is a buying and maintenance cooperative.** Not a commune —
a co-op. It exists for the things one crew of five cannot do alone:

- *A compute pool.* Idle and donated machines, plus capacity rented in bulk and
  metered out. No project dies because someone couldn't afford an hour of GPU.
- *A test bench.* Shared checks that tell you whether a tool actually works, so
  no crew has to invent its own definition of "works."
- *A shelf of failures.* What was tried, what broke, and why. The most valuable
  thing a movement can own, and the thing everyone throws away.
- *A licensing and rights desk.* Model licenses, data rights, privacy, and
  liability answered once and reused, instead of re-argued by every crew.

**Rules of the commons.** Boundaries come first: your local relationships, your
reputation, and your revenue are yours and are not commoned. Everything held in
common has a named maintainer, because shared property without an owner rots,
and goodwill is not a maintenance plan. Access is open to anyone in the
movement who contributes or pays in. What the commons earns goes back into the
commons.

**Money rules.** Money in this movement may pay for compute, hosting,
translation, printing, and childcare. It may not be promised as salaries we
can't sustain, and it may not buy anyone's agenda. Whatever a crew earns is the
crew's — kept, reinvested, or shared on its own terms.

**First ninety days, for a person arriving today:**

1. Week 1 — do not start a project. Find one person whose problem you can
   actually see, and write down what they need in their words.
2. Week 2 — build the smallest thing that helps them. Ugly is fine.
3. Week 3 — put it in their hands. Watch them use it. Say nothing.
4. Week 4 — fix the thing they got wrong, then find the second person.

Then do it again. That is the whole method.

## 7. What we won't become

- **Another report.** Nothing here ends in a PDF recommending that someone
  else act.
- **A supplier to the machine.** We don't build the tools that decide who is
  disposable: résumé screeners, worker surveillance, fraud detection aimed at
  the poor, automated discipline. If it sorts people for the convenience of
  whoever holds power, we don't make it.
- **Free labor for our own replacement.** We don't work for free for the
  people who replaced us.
- **A substitute for a wage.** We won't let "you have purpose now" be the
  answer to "you have no income." Both problems are real and we will say so.
- **Doom.** Despair feels like seriousness and costs nothing. It is the most
  comfortable way to be useless.

## 8. Tensions we have not solved

We would rather name these than pretend.

- Open weights cut both ways. The same freedom that arms us arms whoever wants
  to build the thing that replaces us cheaper.
- Volunteer groups drift toward whoever has spare time — usually people who
  can afford to be unpaid. That is a real filter, and it excludes exactly the
  people this is supposed to be for. We have to work against it deliberately.
- Free tools can undercut the people we are trying to protect. Our own commons,
  if it destroys the last paid work in a trade, is not a victory. We will have
  to be honest about which one we are building.
- "Be useful" can become its own treadmill — a new way to be told you are
  failing.
- We might be wrong about the scale, the speed, or the permanence of what is
  happening. The response to that is to build things that help people whether
  or not we are right.

## 9. What we commit to

I will not wait to be needed.

I will not confuse agreement with action, or talking with building.

I will put something in a real person's hands, and I will be there when it
breaks.

I will use tools that belong to everyone, on problems that belong to everyone,
and I will give away what I make — while keeping what I earn.

I will hold both truths: that people need purpose, and that people need pay.
I will not trade one for the other, and I will not let anyone else do it in my
name.

I will build as if the people I am building for are in the room, because they
are.
